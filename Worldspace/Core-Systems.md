## The Amundsen Resonance Effect — why the war's ending is mechanically one event

Amundsen Tower's true function was never primarily passenger transport — it was a continent-scale energy-regulating flywheel: a continuously circulating stream of pellets at 4 km/s, drawing ~324 GW net with 85% regenerative recovery, smoothing generation/consumption mismatches across the entire Antarctic grid. Its 2812 destruction is therefore not one disaster but a single instant producing several simultaneously:

1. An acute "load-rejection" shock — every generator tuned to a grid containing the Tower's regulating buffer loses that buffer at once, independent of and additional to city-by-city bombing damage.
2. A chronic, permanent, never-recovered loss of grid regulation, persisting unresolved into the game's present — there was never a "recovery" to return to, because the stabilizing element is simply gone.
3. Severing of the inter-subnet Arcanet backbone — the same Tower-regulated grid the network depended on — very likely the direct mechanism behind the Planetary Split Brain.
4. Collapse of the unified Federation Watt currency (see below) — not a slow devaluation, an instant structural loss.
5. Permanent end of the Mountain Pass robot-fabrication site, which ran on the Tower's overflow power.
6. End of all off-world access.

**This means the show is well positioned to dramatize the Tower's actual destruction on-screen as a single climactic sequence with clearly traceable, cascading consequences — not an abstract offscreen catastrophe.**

Other, less acute contributing factors to the grid's ongoing post-war failure (16-point list in the source, useful as texture rather than plot): structural damage to the Power Core itself; the Antarctic environment actively undoing repairs; resource scarcity; political/factional gridlock; institutional knowledge loss (engineers evacuated off-world or died; even Calethina has gaps in her archives); hidden systemic issues; philosophical/cultural resistance (some factions view the failing grid as spiritually significant); "Ghost Protocol" emergency AI safeguards embedded during the war itself, now too entangled to safely remove; contaminated geothermal wells from wartime weapons; interdependent infrastructure debt; economic/factional vested interests profiting from instability.

## The economy — one currency for the whole show, then it breaks

For virtually the entire Southern Lights window (once Amundsen Tower comes online, ~2630s–2812), Tepenia runs on a single trusted, energy-backed national currency, working title **the Federation Watt** — redeemable for a fixed amount of guaranteed grid capacity. It functions as real, trusted money specifically because Amundsen Tower made the underlying grid genuinely unified and national. It bridges the human/robot dual economy — both human (heating/food/water) and robot (siligel/component maintenance) systems trace back to the same regulated national grid, even though what each population spends it on is completely different.

**The Fracture Point (2812) is not a separate event** — it's the same electrical instant as the Tower's destruction. An energy-backed currency requires (1) a genuinely unified grid to back it and (2) a way to verify that backing across its territory. The Tower's destruction removes both simultaneously — the grid fragments, and the Arcanet backbone that could verify cross-subnet currency claims goes down in the same event. The currency does not slowly devalue over subsequent decades; it loses both structural supports instantly, in the same catastrophe that ends space access and severs the subnets informationally. Post-2812 (game-present): the currency fractures into regional/subnet-level energy scrip plus a separate, harder cross-subnet trade standard used by highway freight traders. **A clean, dramatizable before/after economic marker for the show's ending.**

## City Logistics — how Concordia actually runs day to day

Concordia is a fully enclosed, radial 13-district city (centered on the Hub) connected by three highways (Hwy 110, 37, 183) and an underground Undergrid. **Food:** hydroponics (backbone of human food, entirely power-dependent) + algae/microalgae bioreactors; cold-adapted livestock (yaks primary — yak milk the norm, cow's milk a luxury, beef a status item); aquaculture despite being landlocked; external supply via highway freight only, supplemental at best. **Siligel** synthesized in Capricorn — lethal to humans, entirely separate supply chain. **Water:** not scarce but energy-expensive (ice melted via waste heat, closed-loop recycling). **Energy:** geothermal (Aries) + nuclear + katabatic wind, on a central grid that is "the central crisis of the game," failing in one cascading direction: grid failure → heating/lighting failure → hydroponic/water processing failure → food/water shortage. **Transport:** transit tubes (fast, high energy cost), Rastra shuttles (descended from Belgrano's founding-era Arrastradora, itself inspired by real Soviet Kharkovchanka Antarctic vehicles), Undergrid foot travel. **Manufacturing:** Capricorn (siligel, robot components), Aquarius (experimental tech, cold-resistance testing, the University of Concordia). **Health:** cold injury, altitude sickness, UV/vitamin-D deficiency, and "polar night depression" during the 3-month dark season are named, load-bearing conditions. **Polar calendar:** ~3 months total darkness (Apr–Jul) = maximum heating+lighting load and peak psychological strain; ~3 months midnight sun (Nov–Jan) = managed sleep cycles and a psychological lift; transition months = peak highway/trade activity.

## Enneagram as the universal character engine

Every named character in the source GDD is typed against the classic Riso & Hudson Enneagram, with fixed integration (health) and disintegration (stress) arrows used consistently across writing:

| Type | Integrates toward (health) | Disintegrates toward (stress) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7 — spontaneous, joyful | 4 — moody, irrational |
| 2 | 4 — self-nurturing, aware | 8 — aggressive, dominating |
| 3 | 6 — cooperative, committed | 9 — disengaged, apathetic |
| 4 | 1 — objective, principled | 2 — overinvolved, clinging |
| 5 | 8 — self-confident, decisive | 7 — hyperactive, scattered |
| 6 | 9 — relaxed, optimistic | 3 — competitive, arrogant |
| 7 | 5 — focused, profound | 1 — perfectionistic, critical |
| 8 | 2 — open-hearted, caring | 5 — secretive, fearful |
| 9 | 3 — self-developing, energetic | 6 — anxious, worried |

Edge case: under *extreme* disintegration a character may paradoxically draw on the positive qualities of their stress point as a last-resort coping mechanism; under *extreme* over-integration a character may slide into the unhealthy qualities of their integration point. **Directly reusable for the show:** the same framework can track how a 248-year-old robot behaves "at their best" versus under the accumulated stress of two and a half centuries, while keeping successive generations of human characters behaviorally distinct using the same shared vocabulary.

## One binding design rule worth keeping: unmarked discovery content

The source GDD's deepest lore about robot origins (the Cradle network, its history, specific character connections) is deliberately never gated behind a trackable quest or marker — discoverable only through environmental storytelling, incidental dialogue, and cross-referenced attentiveness. Modeled on Fallout: New Vegas's unmarked quests but goes further. Rationale: "the reward is understanding itself, not a mechanical payoff." **Worth keeping as a tonal model for the show** — its biggest founding-era revelations (the coerced Treaty signature, the Great Corruption) may land better *unmarked*, earned by a viewer's attention rather than delivered as an explicit plot beat.
